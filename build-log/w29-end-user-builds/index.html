The Best Clinical AI PM Was Never a PM

The winners’ list of a recent Claude Code hackathon reads like a waiting room, not an engineering team.

First place went to a California personal-injury lawyer who built a tool for building-permit applications. On the podium with him: a Belgian chief of cardiology, an infrastructure engineer from Uganda. (The full list is here.) The tempting headline is “no developers won” — but that’s wrong; a software engineer took silver. The real story is quieter and more useful: the top prizes went to people who had lived a specific problem for years, and finally had the tools to solve it themselves.

You can watch the same thing happen at kitchen-table scale. A father, frustrated that a cram school was drowning his daughter in TOEFL vocabulary, built her a personalized English app instead — reading passages, listening practice, auto-generated quizzes — with GenAI and vibe coding. He didn’t out-engineer the academy. He out-knew the problem.

The pedigree we keep hiring for

Clinical AI hiring still optimizes for the wrong résumé. We look for the PM who shipped consumer apps, the ML lead with the benchmark wins. The person we skip is the one who spent twenty years on the receiving end of the tools — the technologist who ran the exam, the nurse who lived the workflow.

That’s backwards. When the cost of building collapses, writing the software stops being the scarce ability. Knowing exactly which three seconds of the morning the software has to survive: that stays scarce.

Twenty years as the end user

I spent two decades in a nuclear medicine department before I built anything. When I finally built a radiopharmaceutical dose calculator, the first screen didn’t ask for the textbook formula. It asked for the three things the isotope delivery actually tells you when it arrives — activity, production time, expiration time — in that order, because that’s the order the morning hands them to you.

No PM course teaches that sequence. Twenty years of mornings do. The calculator is now part of the department’s daily routine, and the math had nothing to do with it. The tool was shaped by someone who had already lost that argument with the workflow a hundred times.

The best clinical AI PM was never a PM. They were the end user, for long enough to know where it hurts.
